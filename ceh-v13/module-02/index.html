CEH v13 Module 2: Footprinting & Reconnaissance

Footprinting is the first phase of ethical hacking — gathering information about a target before launching an attack. This module covers passive and active techniques, OSINT tools, WHOIS lookups, DNS interrogation, Google hacking with dorks, and online intelligence services.

Key Topics Covered

  • Passive vs active footprinting techniques
  • OSINT (Open Source Intelligence) collection and analysis
  • WHOIS protocol and Regional Internet Registries (RIRs)
  • DNS interrogation, reverse DNS lookups, MX records
  • Google hacking with dorks: site:, filetype:, inurl:, intitle:, cache:
  • Reconnaissance tools: Maltego, Recon-ng, FOCA, DMITRY
  • Internet asset discovery: Shodan, Censys, Netcraft
  • Footprinting countermeasures and defensive strategies

Important Terms & Concepts

OSINT: Open-source intelligence — collection and analysis of information from public/open sources. Not related to open-source software.
Passive footprinting: Gathering info without direct contact with the target (e.g., news, WHOIS databases, social media).
Active footprinting: Direct interaction with the target (e.g., nmap scanning, port scanning, DNS queries).
Google Dorks: Advanced Google search operators: site:, filetype:, inurl:, intitle:, intext:, cache:
WHOIS guard: A proxy between domain owners and WHOIS accessers. Emails are usually still redirected, allowing phishing attempts.
MX records: Mail exchange records that expose which email service a target uses — smallest preference number = highest priority.
Maltego: Proprietary OSINT tool that provides graphical links for investigative tasks — visualizing relationships between data.
Shodan: Search engine that finds IoT devices connected to the internet (webcams, routers, servers) with filtering by open ports.

Google Dork Examples

  • site:example.com — limits results to a specified domain
  • filetype:pdf site:example.com — PDF files on a domain
  • intitle:"index of" — finds directory listings
  • inurl:admin — pages with admin in the URL

What is Footprinting & Reconnaissance in Ethical Hacking?

Footprinting and Reconnaissance is the first technical domain of the Certified Ethical Hacker v13 (CEH v13) exam administered by EC-Council. This module covers passive and active information gathering techniques, OSINT (Open Source Intelligence), WHOIS and DNS enumeration, Google hacking with advanced search operators, and dedicated reconnaissance platforms like Shodan, Censys, and Maltego. Understanding footprinting is essential because the quality of intelligence gathered in this phase directly determines the success of all subsequent attack stages. On the CEH v13 exam, questions related to Module 2 account for approximately 8% of total questions.

Key Concepts in Footprinting & Reconnaissance

  • Passive vs Active Footprinting: Passive footprinting gathers information without directly interacting with the target (WHOIS databases, news archives, social media, certificate transparency logs). Active footprinting involves direct contact with target systems (DNS queries, port scans, banner grabbing). The CEH exam frequently asks you to classify a technique as passive or active.
  • DNS Record Types & Interrogation: Ethical hackers query A, AAAA, MX, NS, TXT, SOA, CNAME, and SRV records to map an organization's infrastructure. Reverse DNS lookups (PTR records) can expose hostnames that reveal server roles. Zone transfer (AXFR) vulnerabilities expose the entire DNS zone.
  • OSINT Framework: Open Source Intelligence is the systematic collection of publicly available data — from corporate filings and job postings to geotagged images and dark web forums. Tools like Maltego (graph-based relationship mapping), Recon-ng (framework for recon), FOCA (metadata extraction), and DMITRY (multi-source scanner) automate different OSINT workflows.
  • Shodan & Censys Internet Mapping: Unlike Google which indexes webpages, Shodan and Censys index internet-connected devices by open ports and banners. Shodan can reveal exposed IoT devices, misconfigured routers, and unpatched servers — a critical recon tool for identifying attack surface before any active scanning begins.

Tools Used in Footprinting & Reconnaissance

Ethical hackers use a layered toolkit for information gathering. Primary OSINT platforms include Maltego (visual relationship mapping), Shodan (IoT and port-based internet search), Censys (certificate and banner discovery), and the open-source Recon-ng framework. For DNS interrogation, dig and nslookup are standard; for WHOIS, whois command-line tools query RIR databases. Google hacking relies on advanced operators like site:, filetype:, inurl:, intitle:, and intext:. Hands-on practice with these tools in a legal context — using your own domain or authorized penetration testing engagements — is strongly recommended.

Frequently Asked Questions About Footprinting & Reconnaissance

What is the difference between passive and active footprinting on the CEH exam?

Passive footprinting collects information from third-party sources without any direct interaction with the target system — examples include WHOIS lookups, social media scraping, news archives, and searching Shodan/Censys databases. Active footprinting involves sending packets directly to the target — examples include DNS zone transfers, port scanning, banner grabbing, and traceroute. The CEH exam expects you to identify which category a specific technique falls into, as passive techniques are harder for targets to detect.

Why is Google dorking considered such an effective reconnaissance technique?

Google dorking leverages advanced search operators to extract sensitive information that organizations have inadvertently exposed publicly. A single query like site:target.com filetype:pdf intext:confidential can reveal internal documents, while inurl:admin finds misconfigured administrative interfaces. Because the data is already indexed by Google's crawler, dorking is entirely passive — the target receives no notification. This makes it one of the highest-value reconnaissance techniques with the lowest detection risk.
