Module 1: Introduction to Ethical Hacking

Section 1: What is Ethical Hacking?

Ethical hacking is the practice of systematically and legally accessing computer systems and networks to identify and address security vulnerabilities before malicious attackers can exploit them.

Key Characteristics

  • Authorized: Written permission from system owner
  • Legal: Complies with applicable laws and regulations
  • Ethical: Conducted with integrity and professionalism
  • Purposeful: Aims to improve security posture, not cause harm

Goals of Ethical Hacking

  1. Test and evaluate the security of information systems
  2. Identify vulnerabilities before malicious hackers exploit them
  3. Ensure compliance with security policies and regulations
  4. Protect sensitive data from unauthorized access

Section 2: Types of Hackers

White Hat Hackers

Ethical hackers who perform penetration testing with explicit permission. They follow a code of ethics and work to improve security. Often employed as security consultants or internal security teams.

Black Hat Hackers

Malicious hackers who exploit vulnerabilities for personal gain, theft, or damage. They operate without authorization and violate laws and ethical standards.

Grey Hat Hackers

Individuals who may hack without permission but without malicious intent. They might identify vulnerabilities and report them, but their initial access was still unauthorized.

Skinny Hat / Red Hat Hackers

Red Hat: Attacks black hats directly, often using aggressive methods. Operates outside traditional ethical boundaries.

Green Hat: Newbie hackers who are eager to learn but lack experience.

Other Hacker Types

  • Suicide Hackers: Act on ideological or political beliefs, often causing significant damage
  • Hacktivists: Hack for political/ideological motives (e.g., Anonymous)
  • State-Sponsored Hackers: Work for government intelligence agencies
  • Insider Threats: Employees or contractors who misuse their access

Section 3: Ethical Hacking vs. Penetration Testing

Penetration testing is a subset of ethical hacking focused specifically on simulating real-world attacks to find exploitable vulnerabilities.

Key Differences

  • Ethical Hacking: Broader scope, includes social engineering, physical security, and more
  • Penetration Testing: Focused on exploiting vulnerabilities to gain access

Types of Penetration Tests

  • Black Box: Tester has no prior knowledge of the system (simulates external attacker)
  • White Box: Full knowledge of the system including source code and architecture
  • Grey Box: Partial knowledge (e.g., user-level credentials)

Section 4: The Penetration Testing Lifecycle

Penetration testing follows a structured methodology consisting of five phases:

Phase 1: Reconnaissance (Passive & Active)

Gathering information about the target system. Passive reconnaissance uses publicly available information without directly interacting with the target. Active reconnaissance involves direct interaction with the target system.

  • Passive: Whois lookups, DNS enumeration, social media, Google dorking
  • Active: Port scanning, service enumeration, network mapping

Phase 2: Scanning

Using tools to identify open ports, services, and potential vulnerabilities in the target system.

  • Network scanning: Nmap, Netcat
  • Vulnerability scanning: Nessus, OpenVAS
  • Web application scanning: Burp Suite, Nikto

Phase 3: Gaining Access

Exploiting identified vulnerabilities to gain access to the target system. This is where the actual penetration testing occurs.

  • Network service exploitation
  • Web application attacks
  • Social engineering attacks
  • Physical security breaches

Phase 4: Maintaining Access

Determining if the attacker can maintain persistent access to the compromised system. This tests whether an attacker could establish backdoors or persistence mechanisms.

  • Installing backdoors
  • Creating user accounts
  • Establishing command-and-control channels

Phase 5: Covering Tracks & Reporting

Covering tracks: Understanding how attackers hide their activities (log clearing, steganography). Reporting: Documenting findings, vulnerabilities exploited, and recommendations for improvement.

Section 5: Laws and Regulations

Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA)

Primary federal law in the U.S. that criminalizes unauthorized access to computer systems. Enacted in 1986 and amended several times since.

Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA)

Requires financial institutions to explain their information-sharing practices and safeguard sensitive data.

Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)

Sets standards for protecting sensitive patient health information. Applies to healthcare providers, health plans, and healthcare clearinghouses.

Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS)

Security standard for organizations handling branded credit cards. Required by all merchants and service providers.

Sarbanes-Oxley Act (SOX)

Corporate reform law requiring accurate and timely financial reporting. Includes provisions for IT general controls.

General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)

EU regulation on data protection and privacy. Applies to all organizations processing EU citizens' personal data. Fines up to 4% of annual global revenue.

"With great power comes great responsibility." &mdash; When you find vulnerabilities, responsible disclosure is the ethical choice.

Section 6: Ethical Hacking Certifications

EC-Council Certifications

  • CEH (Certified Ethical Hacker): Industry-standard certification for ethical hackers. Covers hacking methodologies, tools, and techniques.
  • CHFI (Computer Hacking Forensic Investigator): Focuses on computer forensics and investigation techniques.
  • CESA (Certified Enterprise Security Analyst): For enterprise-level security analysis.
  • CPTS (Certified Penetration Testing Specialist): Advanced penetration testing certification by Hack The Box.

Other Popular Certifications

  • OSCP (Offensive Security Certified Professional): Hands-on penetration testing certification
  • GPEN (GIAC Penetration Tester): GIAC's penetration testing certification
  • eWPT (eLearnSecurity Web Penetration Tester): Web application penetration testing
  • OSWE (Offensive Security Web Expert): Advanced web application exploitation

Section 7: Hacking Methodology & Ethics

The Three C's of Ethical Hacking

  1. Contract: Always have written authorization before testing
  2. Control: Maintain control over the scope and methodology
  3. Confidentiality: Protect all findings and client data

Rules of Engagement

  • Define the scope of the engagement clearly
  • Establish testing windows and time restrictions
  • Identify critical systems that must not be impacted
  • Define prohibited actions and techniques
  • Establish communication channels and escalation procedures

Code of Ethics

  • Provide honest and diligent service
  • Protect privacy and confidentiality
  • Act in the public interest
  • Advance the profession
  • Maintain professional competence

Section 8: Tools of the Trade

Reconnaissance Tools

  • Shodan: Search engine for Internet-connected devices
  • Nmap: Network discovery and security auditing tool
  • theHarvester: Email, subdomain, names, and ports gathering
  • Maltego: Data mining and link analysis for investigations

Scanning Tools

  • Nmap: Port scanning, service detection, OS fingerprinting
  • Masscan: Internet-wide port scanner
  • Netcat: Network utility for reading/writing network connections

Exploitation Tools

  • Metasploit Framework: Exploitation framework with extensive payload support
  • Burp Suite: Web application security testing platform
  • SQLmap: SQL injection and database takeover tool

Section 9: Types of Security Assessments

Vulnerability Assessment

Identifies and classifies security weaknesses. Non-intrusive in nature. Provides a list of vulnerabilities found.

Risk Assessment

Evaluates the potential impact of identified vulnerabilities on the organization's operations and assets.

Penetration Testing

Simulates real-world attacks to determine if vulnerabilities can be exploited. More intrusive and comprehensive than vulnerability assessment.

Red Team Exercise

Full-scope adversarial simulation testing an organization's overall security posture including people, processes, and technology.
